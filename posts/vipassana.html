Ten Days of Silence: My Vipassana experience

A personal journal from ten days at Dhamma Malaya Vipassana Centre — learning to sit, observe, and simply be with myself.

I had no idea what to expect before going for Vipassana. I knew there would be ten days of silence, meditation, and no connection to the outside world. But what would it actually feel like to spend ten days with nothing but myself?

And so, on 7 October, I left my phone behind and stepped into ten days of a completely different world.

Before leaving, I wrote down three simple things I wanted to practise:

Day 1 — Entering a Different World

No phone. No internet. No talking. No physical contact. No exercise. No Yoga.

For ten days, there would be no messages to reply to, no social media to scroll through, and no connection to the outside world. Life would follow a simple routine: meditate, eat, rest, and repeat.

More than ten hours of meditation every day.

It sounds simple enough when written down, but actually experiencing it is another thing. Sitting for one to two hours at a time, with very little external stimulation, made me realise how much there was to observe, even when I was doing almost nothing.

There was the breath, the body, the mind, and all the little things that came and went. Thoughts appeared, disappeared, and appeared again. Sometimes I was aware of them; sometimes I found myself following them somewhere else entirely.

Without the usual distractions, there was nowhere else to direct my attention. For once, I had no choice but to spend time with myself.

Annapana - Learning to See

The first part of the practice focused on observing the breath. Not controlling it, not deliberately making it longer or deeper, but simply noticing it as it was.

This was interesting because I had previously been curious about breathing practices, including equal breathing and lengthening the breath. My instinct was to do something with the breath — to adjust it, improve it, or make it feel a certain way.

But this time, the practice was different. I was not there to change the experience. I was there to observe it.

It sounds like a small distinction, but I began to notice how quickly the mind wanted to interfere. Even when the instruction was simply to pay attention, there was an urge to make something happen.

And when my mind wandered, I would notice and return to the breath. Again and again.

There was no need to get frustrated about wandering, or to congratulate myself when I managed to concentrate. The practice was simply to notice what had happened and begin again.

Day 4 — Adhiṭṭhāna

From the fourth day, we began practising Adhiṭṭhāna, or strong determination sittings. During these sittings, we were encouraged to remain still for an hour without opening our eyes, changing our posture, or moving our hands and legs.

Sitting still for an hour felt very different from spending an hour doing something else. Without movement or distraction, even the smallest sensations became noticeable. There was discomfort, pain, the urge to move, and the anticipation of how much longer I might have to sit.

The mind could turn a simple sensation into a whole conversation. A little discomfort appeared, and suddenly there were questions: Should I move? Is it getting worse? How much longer is left?

I began to notice that there was the physical sensation itself, and then there was my reaction to it. The sensation was one thing; the resistance, impatience, and desire for it to disappear were something else.

Of course, this was not about ignoring the body's needs or forcing myself to endure pain at all costs. Taking care of the body still matters. But I became curious about what happened when I paused long enough to observe an urge before automatically responding to it.

Sometimes I could remain still a little longer. Sometimes I needed to adjust. Either way, there was an experience to observe.

The Long Hours

One thing I will remember about these ten days is how much time there was.

In everyday life, a day can disappear so quickly. There are things to finish, messages to answer, places to go, and all the little activities that fill the spaces between them. Even when we have free time, it is easy to reach for something to occupy it.

At the retreat, the schedule was structured, but the activities were repetitive. There was no need to decide what to do next. The next meditation, the next meal, the next rest period — everything was already arranged.

And yet, doing very little externally did not mean that nothing was happening. The mind continued to wander, the body continued to experience different sensations, and my attention shifted from one thing to another.

It made me think about how accustomed I am to filling empty moments. When I feel bored, uncomfortable, or uncertain, there is usually something available to distract me. A phone, a book, a conversation, another task.

Here, those options were taken away.

I had to sit with the experience itself, even when there was nothing particularly interesting or meaningful about it.

Eating, Walking, and Taking Care of My Body

Before the retreat, I had written down that I wanted to eat mindfully, walk mindfully, sit mindfully, and take care of my body.

These were simple intentions, but I like how they brought my attention back to ordinary activities. Breathing, eating, walking, resting — things that happen every day, often without much thought.

We spend so much time thinking about what comes next that we can easily overlook what is already happening. We eat while thinking about the next task, walk while looking at our phones, and rest while feeling that we should be doing something more productive.

I wanted to explore what it would feel like to give these ordinary moments a little more attention.

I was also reminded that taking care of the body does not always mean pushing it further. Sometimes it means moving; sometimes it means staying still. Sometimes it means challenging ourselves, and sometimes it means recognising our limits.

Perhaps taking care of ourselves begins with noticing what is actually needed, instead of always following what the mind wants.

Ten Days Without Talking

There was something unusual about being around other people without speaking to them. We shared the same space, followed the same routine, and sat through the same meditation sessions, yet each person was having their own experience.

There were no ordinary conversations, no casual updates about life, and no immediate opportunity to explain what I was thinking or feeling. I could not compare experiences as they unfolded or seek reassurance from someone else.

I simply had to experience things for myself.

It made me reflect on how much of everyday life involves expressing, explaining, and responding. We often want to put an experience into words as soon as it happens, perhaps to understand it better or to share it with someone.

But not everything needs to be explained immediately. Sometimes, it is enough to allow an experience to unfold without deciding what it means.

Returning to the Outside World

After ten days, it was time to return to ordinary life.

The messages, notifications, responsibilities, and routines were still there. The world had continued moving while I was away, and there was no expectation that everything would suddenly feel different simply because I had spent ten days meditating.

I did not return with every question answered, nor did I expect to become someone who was always calm, patient, and mindful. I am still the same person, with the same habits, interests, uncertainties, and things to figure out.

But I now have ten days of experience to look back on. Ten days of observing the breath, sitting with discomfort, noticing the mind wander, and practising the simple act of paying attention.

What interests me now is what happens after the retreat. Can I remember to pause before reacting? Can I eat without rushing, rest without feeling guilty, or notice when my body needs something different from what I had planned?

I suppose these are things I will only find out with time.

One Moment at a Time

I do not think I need to turn these ten days into a grand lesson or find a meaningful takeaway for every moment. Some experiences can simply be experienced. Some things take time to understand.

Before going, I had no idea what I would feel or what I would bring home with me. I still do not have all the answers, and perhaps that is okay.

For now, I am grateful that I gave myself the opportunity to pause — to step away from the usual routines and spend some time observing what was already there.

Maybe the practice does not end when we leave the meditation hall. Perhaps it continues in the ordinary moments of everyday life: in the way we breathe, eat, walk, rest, listen, and respond.

Not needing every moment to be special. Not expecting every experience to teach us something immediately. Just being willing to pay attention to what is happening, one moment at a time.

Bhavatu sabba maṅgalaṃ.
May all beings be happy.

Sādhu, sādhu, sādhu.
