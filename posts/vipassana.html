Ten Days of Silence: My Vipassana experience

Ten days at Dhamma Malaya Vipassana Centre — learning to sit, observe, and simply be with myself.

I had no idea what to expect before going for Vipassana. I knew there would be ten days of silence, meditation, and no connection to the outside world. But what would it actually feel like to spend ten days with nothing but myself?

And so, on 7 October, I left my phone behind and stepped into ten days of a completely different world.

The Journey Within: The Art of Observing

Before the meditation began, we were asked to observe the Five Precepts throughout the ten days. These included refraining from killing any living being, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants. A long with noble silence and the absence of external distractions, these precepts created an environment for practising mindfulness, discipline, and self-observation.

More than ten hours of meditation every day. The schedule began at 4:00 a.m. and ended at 9:00 p.m., with the day structured around meditation, meals, and short breaks.

4:00 a.m. — Morning wake-up
4:30 – 6:30 a.m. — Meditation
6:30 – 8:00 a.m. — Breakfast and rest
8:00 – 11:00 a.m. — Meditation
11:00 a.m. – 1:00 p.m. — Lunch and rest
1:00 – 5:00 p.m. — Meditation
5:00 – 6:00 p.m. — Tea break and rest
6:00 – 7:00 p.m. — Meditation
7:00 – 8:15 p.m. — Teacher's discourse
8:15 – 9:00 p.m. — Meditation
9:00 p.m. — Retire for the night

It sounds simple enough when written down, but experiencing it was another thing. Sitting for long hours brought discomfort, aches, and pain, along with the constant urge to shift or move. There were moments when staying still felt almost unbearable.

Yet, there was also the breath, the body, the mind, and all the sensations that came and went. Thoughts appeared and disappeared; sometimes I observed them, and sometimes I found myself following them somewhere else entirely.

Without the usual distractions, there was nowhere else to direct my attention. For once, I was simply there with myself, learning to observe discomfort without immediately reacting to it.

Day 1–3: Anapana — Learning to See

The first three days were dedicated to Anapana, the practice of observing the natural breath. We were instructed to bring our attention to the breath as it entered and left the nostrils, noticing its natural sensations without deliberately controlling or changing it. The practice was not about regulating the breath, but about training the mind to remain attentive to what was already happening.

This was interesting because I had previously been curious about breathing practices, including equal breathing and lengthening the breath. My instinct was to do something with the breath — to adjust it, improve it, or make it feel a certain way.

But this time, the practice was different. I was not there to change the experience. I was there to observe it.

It sounds like a small distinction, but I began to notice how quickly the mind wanted to interfere. Even when the instruction was simply to pay attention, there was an urge to make something happen.

Through Anapana, I began learning to recognise this tendency and gently return my attention to the natural breath, again and again.

Day 4 — Adhitthana

From the fourth day, we began practising Adhitthana (strong determination sittings). During these sittings, we were to remain still for an hour without opening our eyes, changing our posture, or moving our hands and legs.

Sitting still for an hour felt very different from spending an hour doing something else. Without movement or distraction, even the smallest sensations became noticeable. There was discomfort, pain, the urge to move, and the anticipation of how much longer I might have to sit.

The mind could turn a simple sensation into a whole conversation. A little discomfort appeared, and suddenly there were questions: Should I move? How much longer is left?

I began to notice that there was the physical sensation itself, and then there was my reaction to it. The sensation was one thing; the resistance, impatience, and desire for it to disappear were something else.

Of course, this was not about ignoring the body's needs or forcing myself to endure pain at all costs. Taking care of the body still matters. But I became curious about what happened when I paused long enough to observe an urge before automatically responding to it.

Sometimes I could remain still a little longer. Sometimes I needed to adjust. Either way, there was an experience to observe.

Nothing Stays the Same

One of the fundamental teachings of Vipassana is anicca — impermanence.

As S.N. Goenka repeatedly reminded us during the course: Anicca, anicca, anicca. Everything arises and passes away, arises and passes away, arises and passes away.

We may understand this intellectually. We know that everything changes, that nothing lasts forever. But understanding something in the mind is different from experiencing it directly.

During meditation, we observe sensations appearing and disappearing throughout the body. Some are subtle, some intense. Some feel pleasant, others uncomfortable. Yet, as we continue observing, we begin to notice that sensations do not remain exactly the same. They change in intensity, shift, or eventually disappear.

There is the sensation itself, and then there is the mind's reaction to it. There is the pain, and then there is the thought

There is the experience, and then there is our resistance to experiencing it.
Sometimes, it is this resistance that adds another layer of suffering.
So does the life.

Anicca, anicca, anicca.

Everything arises and passes away. We do not need to cling to what feels pleasant, nor immediately push away what feels unpleasant. We learn to observe both with greater equanimity, knowing that neither experience is permanent.

Day 10 — Metta (Loving-kindness) & Karuna (Compassion)

On the tenth day, we were introduced to Metta, or loving-kindness. After days of observing sensations, learning about impermanence, and practising equanimity, the practice turned towards wishing happiness and peace for ourselves and for all beings.

May all beings be happy. May all beings be peaceful. May all beings be free from suffering.

The reminder to share the happiness, peace, and harmony we have cultivated with others. Whatever peace we find within ourselves does not have to remain within us. We can share it through our thoughts, words, and actions, allowing kindness to extend beyond our own experience.

The Vipassana course is offered entirely through dana — voluntary donations given by previous students. We were able to attend the course without paying a fee. The food, accommodation, facilities, and the opportunity to learn and practise are supported by the generosity of others. The teachers and volunteers also contribute their time and effort to make the course possible.

Sometimes, it is our time, attention, effort, or simply the willingness to be there for someone else. We may never fully know how our small acts of kindness affect another person's life.

Perhaps this is one way to carry Metta and Karuna into everyday life: to recognise what we have received, to appreciate the people and conditions that have made our lives possible, and to give when we are able, without always expecting something in return.

Beyond Knowing: Sati, Upekkha & Panna

Vipassana is not simply about sitting still and observing sensations. It is also about learning how to relate to our experiences.

  • Sati (Awareness) – Allows us to notice what is happening in the present moment. Through awareness, we recognise what is happening within and around us.
  • Upekkha (Equanimity) – Invites us to meet pleasant and unpleasant experiences without reacting or being carried away by them. Through equanimity, we learn to see things as they are, rather than as we wish them to be.

Living the life with Bhavana-maya panna - wisdom developed through direct experience and practice. Knowing that everything is impermanent is different from experiencing change for ourselves. Understanding equanimity is different from practising it when life becomes uncomfortable.

The real value of the practice lies in how these teachings extend beyond the meditation hall.
Can we notice frustration before reacting?
Accept uncertainty without immediately seeking control?
Listen with patience and respond with compassion?

After ten days, it was time to return to ordinary life.

The messages, notifications, responsibilities, and routines were still there. The world had continued moving while I was away, and there was no expectation that everything would suddenly feel different simply because I had spent ten days meditating.

I did not return with every question answered, nor did I expect to become someone who was always calm, patient, and mindful. I am still the person, with uncertainties and things to figure out.

Not needing every moment to be special. Not expecting every experience to teach us something immediately. Just being willing to pay attention to what is happening, one moment at a time.

Practice continues in ordinary moments — in the way we think, speak, act, and relate to others. Not about getting everything right, but learning to notice, pause, and begin again.
