The Four Keys to Four Locks

Yoga Sutra 1.33

maitri karuna muditopeksanam sukha duhkha punya apunya visayanam bhavanatas citta prasadanam

“By cultivating attitudes (bhavanatah) of friendliness toward the happy, compassion for the unhappy, delight in the virtuous and disregard toward the wicked, the mind-stuff retains its undisturbed calmnesss (prasadanam).”

Pause for a moment and think: how do you react when you see someone who is happy, unhappy, virtuous, or wicked?

The Four Locks

sukha (happiness) · dukha (sorrow) · punya (virtue) · apunya (non-virtue/wicked)

They are not four types of people, but four things we encounter in human life:
states of experience — sukha and dukha;
ways of acting — punya and apunya.

The sutra is not asking us to classify people according to these four categories. It asks us to notice how we respond when we encounter them.

Why Do We Need Four Keys?

Someone's happiness can awaken jealousy.

Someone's suffering can awaken indifference, discomfort, or avoidance.

Someone's virtue can awaken comparison or envy.

Someone's harmful conduct can awaken anger, hatred, or judgment.

The lock isn't necessarily the person's happiness, suffering, virtue, or harmful conduct.
The lock is what our mind does in response to encountering it.

We can't always choose what we encounter. But perhaps we can learn to notice the reaction it produces in us — and choose how we meet it.

The Four Keys: Learning to Respond, Not React

maitri (friendliness) · karuna (compassion) · mudita (joy) · upeksa (equanimity)

  • Maitri – Friendliness / Loving-kindness
    Application: When I encounter someone else's happiness, can I meet it with genuine friendliness rather than comparison or jealousy?
  • Karuna – Compassion
    Application: When I encounter someone's suffering, can I respond with compassion rather than indifference or avoidance?
  • Mudita – Appreciative Joy
    Application: When I encounter someone's virtue or good fortune, can I genuinely rejoice without comparing it to myself?
  • Upeksha – Equanimity
    Application: Can I discern what is harmful without letting anger, hatred, or judgment take over my mind?

See What Happens Within Us

We often divide our experiences into what feels positive and what feels negative — happiness and suffering, good and bad. And naturally, we develop preferences: we move toward what feels pleasant, pull away from what feels unpleasant, admire what we consider good, and resist what we consider wrong.

Sukha, dukha, punya and apunya offer us an opportunity to notice how we respond. In the way we respond, we catch a glimpse of ourselves — our habitual patterns, preferences, and tendencies. Seeing them more clearly, we can begin to choose a wiser response: friendliness, compassion, appreciative joy, or equanimity.

The practice, then, is not about changing what we see, nor about changing others. It is learning to see what happens within us when we see — to turn our attention inward and notice the movements of our own mind.

And perhaps this brings us back to where the Yoga Sutras begin:

yogas citta vrtti nirodhah

Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind.

Reference

Swami Satchidananda. (1990). The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali
